\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Segmenting organs and lesions in 3D scans such as CT and MRI underpins
computer-aided diagnosis, treatment planning, and quantitative follow-up, where
accuracy at the voxel level directly affects clinical measurements.  This task is
dominated by U-Net--based encoder--decoder networks, and recent progress has come
mostly from stronger but heavier variants.  Transformer-based models such as
UNETR~\cite{hatamizadeh2022unetr} and Swin UNETR~\cite{tang2022swinunetr}, and
large-kernel convolutional models such as 3D UX-Net~\cite{lee20233duxnet},
improve accuracy at a substantial computational cost.  The number of voxels
grows cubically with per-axis resolution; dense 3D convolutions are linear in
this voxel count, while global self-attention is quadratic in it, so memory,
latency, and energy rise rapidly with volume resolution and decoder width.  Even
self-configuring pipelines such as nnU-Net~\cite{isensee2021nnunet} inherit this
burden.

EffiDec3D~\cite{rahman2025effidec3d} targets this cost on the decoder side.  It
\emph{uniformly} reduces decoder channel width and removes high-resolution
decoder stages, cutting decoder parameters by 96.4\% and FLOPs by 93.0\% while
retaining competitive aggregate Dice.  This is strong evidence that decoders are
over-provisioned on average.  However, it shrinks capacity uniformly
\emph{without explaining why} the removed computation was unnecessary, and it
keeps the decoder \emph{static}: the same reduced budget is applied to every
subject, organ, and voxel.  It therefore leaves unanswered a more basic point:
\emph{where} decoder computation is actually needed, and whether that need is
uniform or concentrated.  Aggregate Dice and FLOPs cannot reveal this, because a
difficult or uncertain voxel is not necessarily one a stronger decoder can fix,
and disagreement between decoders is not necessarily improvement --- a full
decoder can also break a prediction that the efficient decoder got right.

We answer this question directly.  On 13-organ abdominal CT, we compare a full 3D
UX-Net decoder (\fullmodel) with its EffiDec3D-compressed counterpart
(\effimodel) under a common dataset, split, preprocessing pipeline, training
schedule, and evaluation protocol.  At every voxel we simply count where the full
decoder \emph{fixes} the efficient decoder's prediction (a \emph{positive
transition}: efficient wrong, full correct) and where it \emph{breaks} it (a
\emph{negative transition}: efficient correct, full wrong), and we test whether
the efficient model's own entropy predicts these locations.  All statistics are
computed at the subject level, so that millions of correlated voxels are not
treated as independent samples.  More specifically, our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A transition-count comparison of efficient and full decoders.}
    Instead of comparing only aggregate Dice and FLOPs, we count per-voxel
    positive and negative transitions and their net difference, with
    subject-level bootstrap, so that not every disagreement is counted as an
    improvement.
    \item \textbf{Evidence that the full decoder's benefit is small,
    bidirectional, and spatially concentrated.}  Net positive transitions amount
    to only about $0.1\%$ of evaluated voxels and are largely offset by negative
    ones; they concentrate at organ boundaries (boundary error $3.93\times$
    interior) and in small organs (size--difficulty $\rho=-0.54$; entropy still
    predicts difficulty after controlling for organ size, partial $r=0.81$).
    Efficient decoders are therefore over-provisioned \emph{non-uniformly}.
    \item \textbf{Evidence that this benefit is predictable at test time.}
    Ranking voxels by the efficient model's entropy places about $86\%$ of
    positive transitions in the top $20\%$ of voxels, well above random, boundary,
    and confidence controls --- identifying where a stronger decoder helps without
    access to ground truth.
\end{itemize}

We deliberately stop short of proposing an adaptive method: whether these fixes
can be realized as measured compute savings is the subject of a separate method
study.  Here our goal is to establish whether the empirical premise for such a
method holds.
