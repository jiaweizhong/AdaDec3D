\begin{abstract}
Efficient 3D segmentation decoders are usually evaluated by aggregate accuracy
and computational cost, which leaves a basic question unanswered: \emph{where}
does additional decoder computation actually improve a prediction?  Using a
controlled reimplementation of a full 3D UX-Net decoder and its compressed
EffiDec3D counterpart on 13-organ abdominal CT, we answer this by directly
counting, at each voxel, where the full decoder \emph{fixes} the efficient
decoder's prediction (a positive transition) and where it \emph{breaks} it (a
negative transition), rather than treating every disagreement as a benefit.
Three findings emerge.  First, errors are spatially concentrated: boundary error
is $3.93\times$ interior error, and difficulty grows as organ size shrinks
($\rho=-0.54$).  Second, the full decoder's net benefit is small and
bidirectional: across two efficient-decoder seeds, positive and negative
transition rates differ by only $0.065$--$0.130\%$ of evaluated voxels, so
efficient decoders are over-provisioned non-uniformly rather than uniformly.
Third, this small benefit is predictable at test time: subject-level
entropy--net correlations are $0.665$ and $0.646$, and a preliminary voxel oracle
places about $86\%$ of positive transitions in the top $20\%$ entropy-ranked
foreground voxels.  These findings motivate selective decoder allocation but do
not by themselves demonstrate realizable savings; we therefore contribute a
paired, subject-bootstrap evaluation protocol based on net transitions and
contiguous 3D regions that separates oracle opportunity from deployable
selection.
