\begin{abstract}
The decoder is an essential module for fusing multi-scale features and restoring spatial
detail in U-Net architectures for 3D medical image segmentation. Recent designs, especially attention-based 
decoders, sharply increase its cost, and efficient methods such as EffiDec3D respond by shrinking high-resolution 
decoder stages---removing $>90\%$ of its parameters and
FLOPs across 3D UX-Net, MedNeXt-M-K3 and SwinUNETR with little loss in Dice.
This success, however, rests on the empirical assumption that decoder computation is
static and globally redundant; it does not explain \emph{why} the computation is
removable, \emph{where} the decoder still matters, or whether those regions can be
identified at test time. We present a systematic characterization of decoder
redundancy along three axes---\emph{heterogeneity}, \emph{concentration}, and
\emph{predictability}---by counting per-voxel prediction \emph{flips} between matched
full and efficient decoders, with a frozen shared-encoder control that attributes the
changes to the decoder alone and a channel/resolution factor decomposition that
separates the two sources of redundancy. We find the full decoder's net benefit is
small and bidirectional (about $0.1\%$ of voxels), spatially concentrated at organ
boundaries and small structures, and identifiable from the efficient model's own
uncertainty---entropy-ranked regions recover its net benefit at roughly five times
the matched-random rate at a fixed budget. These trends are consistent across three
architectures and three datasets,
indicating that decoder redundancy is structured and predictable rather than uniform.
Our analysis provides an evaluation protocol and design guidance for more efficient,
region-adaptive segmentation decoders.
\end{abstract}
