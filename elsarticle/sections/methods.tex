\section{Methods: A Characterization Framework}
\label{sec:methods}

To understand the spatial and functional utility of decoder computation, we design a controlled observation study. We treat a matched full decoder and an efficient decoder as experimental instruments, isolating the effect of decoder capacity on voxel-level predictions.

We define decoder computation as the sequence of upsampling, skip-feature fusion, and post-fusion refinement operations that transform the bottleneck representation into dense high-resolution predictions. In our experimental design, the encoder feature extractor is held fixed, architecture-specific skip features are held fixed, and interventions occur strictly on decoder width (channel count) and depth (stage removal). For stage removal interventions, the associated skip-fusion operations are treated as part of the removed decoder pathway.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
Let $v$ denote a voxel within a 3D medical image. We evaluate two decoder configurations sharing identical encoder features, training protocols, and data splits:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Efficient Decoder ($E_1$)}: Parameter-matched efficient decoder based on the EffiDec3D configuration \cite{effidec3d}, utilizing concatenation for skip aggregation to strictly align with published parameter counts.
    \item \textbf{Full Decoder ($E_0$)}: The standard dense, high-resolution decoder of the respective backbone architecture.
\end{itemize}

For a given voxel $v$, let $y(v)$ be the ground truth label, $\hat{y}_e(v)$ be the prediction from the efficient decoder, and $\hat{y}_f(v)$ be the prediction from the full decoder.

\subsection{Voxel-level Flip Analysis}
Rather than relying solely on aggregate macroscopic metrics like Dice \cite{taha2015metrics}, which average errors globally, we track how the full decoder alters the prediction of the efficient baseline. Drawing upon prediction disagreement and flip characterization principles from model behavior and uncertainty literature \cite{jungoreyes,cmig2025quality,uncertaintyreview,geirhos2020shortcut}, we define three categories of prediction ``flips'' when transitioning from the efficient to the full decoder:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Positive Flip ($P(v)$)}: The full decoder improves a previously incorrect prediction. $P(v) = 1[\hat{y}_e(v) \neq y(v) \wedge \hat{y}_f(v) = y(v)]$.
    \item \textbf{Negative Flip ($N(v)$)}: The full decoder degrades a previously correct prediction. $N(v) = 1[\hat{y}_e(v) = y(v) \wedge \hat{y}_f(v) \neq y(v)]$.
    \item \textbf{Net Benefit ($U(v)$)}: The voxel-wise difference between improvements and degradations. $U(v) = P(v) - N(v)$.
\end{itemize}
The global rates are defined as $R_{pos} = \text{mean}_v P(v)$, $R_{neg} = \text{mean}_v N(v)$, and $R_{net} = R_{pos} - R_{neg}$.

\subsection{Core Metrics}
We operationalize our investigation into structured redundancy through five formal metrics:

\textbf{1. Global Net Benefit}: Evaluates the macro-level necessity of the full decoder. The global net flip rate $R_{net}$ is computed as the expected voxel-wise net benefit over the volume $V$:
\begin{equation}
R_{net} = \frac{1}{|V|} \sum_{v \in V} U(v)
\end{equation}
This indicates whether the full decoder provides a unidirectional advantage ($R_{net} > 0$) or if positive corrections and negative degradations balance each other out ($R_{net} \approx 0$).

\textbf{2. Boundary and Anatomy Resolution}: To evaluate spatial heterogeneity, we analyze the net benefit $U(v)$ as a function of Euclidean distance to the nearest organ boundary, as well as its distribution across anatomical structures.

\textbf{3. Oracle Concentration}: Measures the spatial sparsity of decoder utility, adapting Pareto concentration and selective classification formulations \cite{cmig2025quality,geifman2017selective}. We partition the image volume into 3D sub-blocks $b_r$ and compute the block-level net benefit $B(r) = \sum_{v \in b_r} U(v)$. Sorting blocks by $B(r)$, the fraction of total positive net mass recovered by the top $k\%$ of blocks is defined as:
\begin{equation}
Coverage(k) = \frac{\sum_{r=1}^{k\% \times |B|} \max(B(r), 0)}{\sum_{r=1}^{|B|} \max(B(r), 0)}
\end{equation}
We summarize this curve using $K_{80}$, representing the volume percentage required to recover 80\% of the full decoder's positive net benefit.

\textbf{4. Flip Predictability}: Evaluates whether decoder behavior can be forecast before execution. We compute lightweight inference-time signals from the efficient decoder, such as predictive Shannon entropy \cite{shannon1948mathematical,gal2016dropout,jungoreyes}:
\begin{equation}
H(v) = -\sum_{c=1}^{C} p_c(v) \log p_c(v)
\end{equation}
where $p_c(v)$ is the softmax probability for class $c$. Following standard error detection and calibration protocols \cite{hendrycks2017baseline,guo2017calibration,jungoreyes,cmig2025clinical,cmig2025quality}, we measure predictability via the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC) for predicting both flip location (instability) and flip direction (improvement vs. degradation).

\textbf{5. Selection Gap}: Measures the deployable opportunity for selective computation \cite{geifman2017selective,cmig2025quality}. We compare the net benefit recovered by an entropy-guided block selector against a random selection baseline at a fixed computational budget (e.g., 20\%).
