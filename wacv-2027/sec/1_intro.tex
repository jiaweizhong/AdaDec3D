\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Volumetric medical image segmentation is increasingly driven by heavy
U-Net--based architectures, particularly ones with transformer or large-kernel
decoders that recover fine spatial detail through multi-scale
fusion~\cite{hatamizadeh2022unetr,tang2022swinunetr,lee20233duxnet}.  Their cost
grows rapidly with resolution: the number of voxels grows cubically with the
per-axis resolution, dense 3D convolution scales linearly in that voxel count,
and global self-attention scales quadratically in it.  The decoder, with its
wide, high-resolution stages, often accounts for a large share of this cost,
which limits deployment in real-time and resource-constrained settings.

Efficient-decoder methods reduce this burden.  \method{}~\cite{rahman2025effidec3d}
uniformly shrinks decoder channels and removes the highest-resolution stages,
preserving aggregate accuracy across twelve tasks while cutting decoder
parameters and FLOPs by an order of magnitude.  Such methods answer how much
decoder computation can be removed on average, but not where the removed
computation would have helped.  They apply the same reduction to every subject,
organ, and spatial location, implicitly assuming that decoder capacity is useful
roughly uniformly.  Aggregate Dice and FLOPs cannot test this assumption: they
reveal neither where additional decoder capacity improves a prediction, where it
degrades one, nor whether such changes are spatially concentrated and
predictable.  A location that is hard to segment is not necessarily one a
stronger decoder can fix, and a confident location may still be correctable.

This paper characterizes where the full decoder's computation actually changes
predictions.  We compare a full and an efficient decoder that share an identical
encoder, so that every difference is attributable to decoder capacity, and we
count, per voxel, the \emph{positive transitions} the full decoder produces
(efficient wrong, full correct) and the \emph{negative transitions} it produces
(efficient correct, full wrong).  We then test whether the efficient model's own
uncertainty identifies where the net benefit occurs, using subject-level
statistics throughout.  More specifically, our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A transition-count comparison of efficient and full decoders.}
    Instead of comparing only aggregate Dice and FLOPs, we count per-voxel
    positive, negative, and net transitions with subject-level bootstrap, so that
    not every disagreement is counted as an improvement.
    \item \textbf{Evidence that decoder benefit is small, bidirectional, and
    spatially concentrated.} The net benefit is only about $0.1\%$ of voxels and
    is largely offset by negative transitions, yet it is far from uniform:
    it concentrates at organ boundaries (boundary error $3.93\times$ interior) and
    small organs (size--difficulty $\rho=-0.54$; entropy predicts difficulty
    beyond organ size, partial $r=0.81$).
    \item \textbf{Evidence that this benefit is predictable at test time.} At a
    predeclared $20\%$ budget, ranking contiguous 3D regions by the efficient
    model's entropy captures the full decoder's net benefit at about five times
    the rate of matched-random regions, with a positive paired subject-bootstrap
    lower bound---identifying where a stronger decoder helps without ground truth.
\end{itemize}

We deliberately stop short of proposing an adaptive method: whether these
improvements can be realized as measured compute savings is the subject of a
separate method study.  Our goal here is to establish whether the empirical
premise for such a method holds, and to provide an evaluation protocol for it.
