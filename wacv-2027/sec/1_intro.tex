\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Encoder--decoder U-Nets are the dominant architecture for 3D medical image
segmentation, where the decoder fuses multi-scale encoder features and restores the
spatial detail needed for accurate organ boundaries. As backbones have grown---from
convolutional~\cite{lee20233duxnet,roy2023mednext} to
Transformer~\cite{hatamizadeh2022unetr,tang2022swinunetr,he2023swinunetrv2}
designs---the decoder has become a large share of the compute and memory budget,
particularly its high-resolution stages that operate at or near input resolution.
Efficient-decoder methods respond by shrinking these stages: \method{}, a recent
example, removes more than $85\%$ of the decoder's parameters and FLOPs across 3D
UX-Net, MedNeXt-M-K3, and Swin UNETR while losing little Dice~\cite{rahman2025effidec3d}.

Such results demonstrate that decoder computation is \emph{removable}: one can delete it
and watch aggregate accuracy hold. They do not, however, explain \emph{why} it is
removable, \emph{where} in the volume the full decoder still changes predictions, or
whether those regions can be identified at test time. This gap matters for the field's
next step. If the removed capacity were uniformly useless, efficient decoders could stop
at a fixed reduction; if instead its benefit were concentrated and predictable, one could
allocate decoder computation \emph{adaptively}, spending it only where it helps. Aggregate
Dice and FLOPs cannot distinguish these cases, because a metric that averages over
millions of voxels hides both the spatial structure of the change and its direction.

We therefore complement the ablation evidence with a systematic characterization of
decoder redundancy. Rather than asking whether the decoder can be removed, we ask
\emph{how} its contribution is distributed, organizing the study along three axes:
\textbf{heterogeneity} (is the full decoder's effect uniform, or does it depend on
location and anatomy?), \textbf{concentration} (is the beneficial computation confined to
a small subset of regions?), and \textbf{predictability} (can a test-time signal find
those regions without the ground truth?). Our unit of analysis is the per-voxel
\emph{prediction flip} between a matched full and efficient decoder---the standard tool
for comparing two classifiers on paired data~\cite{yan2021pct,cormier2016churn,mcnemar1947}
---which separates cases where the full decoder \emph{corrects} the efficient one
(positive flips) from those where it \emph{degrades} it (negative flips). We evaluate two
regimes: an end-to-end matched pair, and a frozen shared-encoder control that isolates the
decoder path. Generality is tested with an L-shaped protocol spanning three architectures
(3D UX-Net, Swin UNETR, MedNeXt-M-K3) and three datasets
(BTCV~\cite{antonelli2022msd}, FeTA~\cite{payette2021feta}, and MSD HepaticVessel).

Our characterization yields a nuanced picture that the aggregate numbers obscure. At the
parameter-matched configuration the full decoder's net benefit is
\emph{bidirectional to the point of cancellation}: it corrects and degrades comparable
numbers of voxels, so the net flip rate is statistically indistinguishable from zero
(e.g.\ $-0.001\%$ on Swin UNETR). Yet this voxel-level neutrality coexists with a
$1$--$2$ point Dice gap, because the near-balanced flips concentrate in a thin band within
one to two voxels of organ boundaries---exactly where Dice is most sensitive. Finally,
the efficient model's own uncertainty predicts \emph{where} a prediction will change
(AUROC $\approx\!0.9$) but not \emph{whether} the change is an improvement (direction
AUROC $\approx\!0.5$): predicting instability is easy, predicting improvement is not. This
last result is the central obstacle to turning decoder redundancy into a deployable,
benefit-aware efficient decoder, and it holds consistently across the three architectures.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
  \item We reframe efficient-decoder evaluation from ``can the computation be removed?''
  to ``where and why is it removable?'', and give an evaluation protocol---prediction
  flips resolved by boundary distance and anatomy, an oracle concentration analysis, and
  flip-prediction AUROC---that exposes what aggregate Dice and FLOPs hide.
  \item We characterize the redundancy empirically: the full decoder's net voxel benefit
  is statistically indistinguishable from zero yet Dice-relevant through a thin boundary
  band, and this net-neutral-but-boundary-localized pattern is consistent across three
  architectures and three datasets.
  \item We show that the beneficial flips are predictable in \emph{location} but not in
  \emph{direction}, quantify the resulting ceiling on test-time region selection, and draw
  concrete implications for designing and auditing region-adaptive decoders.
\end{itemize}
